Operation Sovereign Sky: Facilitator Debrief Guide

Use these notes to debrief cadets after each event or at the end of each stage. Every event directly demonstrates core concepts from the RAFAC Senior & Master Air Cadet Air Power syllabus.

Pre-Game Setup: Variant Axes

  • Basing & Carrier Posture: Carrier Enabled Power Projection (HMS Queen Elizabeth) increases F-35B time-on-station without Air-to-Air Refuelling and extends reach, but limits total force to what the carrier can hold. Operating from an overseas base (RAF Akrotiri) reduces aircraft vulnerability in theatre and increases flexibility, but requires greater reach and tanker support.
  • Adversary Profile: Symmetrical Warfare pits forces of comparable military power and resources against each other, whereas Asymmetrical Warfare involves a major difference in power and tactics (e.g., insurgents using mobile drones/MANPADS and human shields).

Stage 1: ISR, Enablers & Deployment

  • 1.1 National Security & Preventative Diplomacy (ev_1): Air Power supports the National Security Strategy via Protect (protecting UK territory/airspace), Project (projecting global influence rapidly), and Promote (alliances/prosperity). Respect the 3 Levels of Warfare: Strategic (Government aims), Operational (JFAC theatre campaign planning), and Tactical (squadron sorties). Visible flypasts achieve Conflict Prevention / Defence Diplomacy and Deterrence. Q: Why shouldn't strategic politicians micro-manage tactical cockpit headings?
  • 1.2 Hybrid Threats: OSINT, FININT & HUMINT (ev_2): Distinguishes State Actors vs Non-State Actors. Tracking smuggling requires fusing OSINT (social media), FININT (financial transactions), and HUMINT (informants) alongside economic action. Q: How can social media posts accidentally give OSINT to an enemy?
  • 1.3 Basing & Fragility Dilemma (ev_3): Tests the limitation of Vulnerability / Fragility on the ground. Basing 40 miles from enemy artillery invites destruction; basing at RAF Akrotiri protects the fleet while Voyager AAR mitigates the extra Reach required. Q: What is the trade-off between basing close to the battle vs 150 miles away?
  • 1.4 Enabling Air Power: Hub & Spoke (ev_4): Demonstrates the 4 Enablers: Base, Sustain (Air Logistics), Connect (RAF Signals), and Protect (RAF Regiment). Links the UK Hub (Brize Norton) to the forward Spoke (Akrotiri) using Inter-Theatre strategic airlift (C-17 / A400M) rather than short-range Intra-Theatre helicopters. Q: What happens if we deploy fast jets without the Connect and Protect enablers?
  • 1.5 The 9-Step ISR Cycle & NEC (ev_5): Contrasts Surveillance (continuous wide-area observation) with Reconnaissance (targeted specific observation). Uses Rivet Joint (SIGINT/MASINT) and Reaper/Shadow R1 (GEOINT) across the 9-step ISR cycle, disseminating data via Link-16 Network Enabled Capability (NEC)—getting the right info to the right place at the right time. Q: What is the difference between Surveillance and Reconnaissance?

Stage 2: Control of the Air (DCA & OCA)

  • 2.1 Defensive Counter-Air: Active & Passive (ev_6): Active DCA detects, intercepts, and engages incoming threats (Typhoon QRA + Type 45 Sea Viper SAMs). Passive DCA reduces ground vulnerability via Hardening (shelters), Dispersal, Camouflage, Concealment, and Deception (decoys). Q: Why do we need Passive DCA shelters even when Typhoons are airborne?
  • 2.2 Asymmetric Drone Swarm (ev_7): Insurgents use Asymmetrical Warfare to hit fragile aircraft on the ground. The RAF Regiment defends the exterior footprint (Protect enabler) while Electronic Warfare jams drone links. Q: Why is a £500 drone a major threat to a £90m fighter jet?
  • 2.3 Height, Perspective & Ubiquity (ev_8): Height provides Perspective (extended radar horizon into mountain valleys). Combining Height, Speed, and Reach creates Ubiquity. High-altitude AEW&C and stratospheric solar platforms (Zephyr) look down over terrain barriers. Q: How does altitude change what a sensor can see compared to a ship radar?
  • 2.4 Offensive Counter-Air & Op Orchard Cyber (ev_9): OCA destroys enemy SAMs and aircraft at their source. Mirrors Operation Orchard (2007): using Offensive Air-Cyber Integration to blind enemy air defence radars so F-35Bs and Typhoons can penetrate A2AD airspace safely. Q: Why destroy enemy planes on their runways (OCA) rather than over our base (DCA)?
  • 2.5 Cementing Air Superiority / Supremacy (ev_10): Reviews the 3 Degrees of Control of the Air: Favourable Air Situation -> Air Superiority -> Air Supremacy (enemy incapable of effective interference). Uses Battlefield Air Interdiction (BAI) against SAM resupply convoys to secure Air Supremacy. Q: Can a C-17 or Chinook operate safely if we only have a Favourable Air Situation?

  • 3.1 Counter-Sea & Air-Maritime Integration (ev_11): Counter-Sea covers Anti-Submarine Warfare (ASW) and Anti-Surface Warfare (ASuW). Pairs the RAF P-8A Poseidon (sonobuoys/torpedoes) with the RN Wildcat helicopter and HMS Duncan via Air-Maritime Integration (AMI).   • 3.3 Weather & Limited Payload (ev_13): The 3 factors determining Air Attack success are Penetration, Payload (number of weapons carried), and Weather.   • 3.4 Agility, Speed & Dynamic Re-Tasking (ev_14): Demonstrates Speed, Reach, Agility, and Flexibility/Versatility by dynamically re-tasking airborne multi-role Typhoon FGR4s via Link-16 to intercept a fleeting target 60 miles away in minutes. Q: Why is a multi-role/swing-role aircraft so valuable to JFAC?
  • 3.5 Information Activities: EW & PsyOps (ev_15): The 4th category of Air Attack is Information Activities: non-kinetic Electronic Warfare (jamming) and Psychological Operations (leaflet drops as in Op Shader, radio broadcasts, RPAS presence, fast-jet Shows of Force) to compel surrender without bombing a hospital. Q: Why are Information Activities classed under Air Attack even when no bombs fall?

  • 4.2 Impermanence & Air-to-Air Refuelling (ev_17): Aircraft suffer from Impermanence (they cannot stay airborne forever). Air-to-Air Refuelling (AAR) via Voyager overcomes impermanence and extends Reach and Endurance—just like the Victor K2 tankers in Operation Black Buck (1982). Q: Why did Op Black Buck require 11 Victor tankers for a single Vulcan bomber?
  • 4.3 Intra-Theatre Mobility: JHC Helicopters (ev_18): Uses Joint Helicopter Command (JHC) Chinook (heavy underslung loads) and Puma HC2 helicopters for vertical Intra-Theatre lift into mountain terrain where fixed-wing planes cannot land. Q: What is the difference between Inter-Theatre and Intra-Theatre mobility?
  • 4.4 Personnel Recovery & 3-Tier Medevac (ev_19): Contrasts Peacetime Recovery with Combat Recovery (time-sensitive rescue expecting hostile interference, as in Exercise Volfa). Demonstrates the 3 tiers of Aeromedical Evacuation: Forward Medevac (battlefield to initial care via Puma/Chinook MERT) -> Tactical Medevac (between theatre hospitals via A400M) -> Strategic Medevac (theatre to the UK via C-17). Q: Name the three tiers of Aeromedical Evacuation in order from battlefield to the UK.

Stage 5: Multi-Domain & Strategic Endgame

  • 5.1 Air-Space Integration & PNT Jamming (ev_20): Air-Space Integration provides vital Positioning, Navigation, and Timing (PNT / GPS) for aircraft and weapons, plus SATCOM voice/data links. Countering enemy GPS Denial/Disruption requires space resilience and destroying the jammers. Q: What systems on a modern fighter fail if space satellites are jammed?
  • 5.2 Strategic Attack, Joint Action & Full Spectrum (ev_21): Strategic Attack targets core regime C2 to compel capitulation (Coercion). Lasting peace (Post-Conflict Peace Building) requires Joint Operations (because aircraft cannot occupy land alone) and a Full Spectrum Approach (Military, Diplomatic, and Economic). Q: Why can't the RAF win a war and hold territory entirely on its own?

Station 1: JFAC Commander (11 Group)

Your Role: You command the UK Joint Force Air Component (UK JFAC), providing scalable and deployable operational-level Command and Control (C2) for all UK air operations in theatre.

1. Core Air Power Definitions

  • Air Power: "The ability to use air capabilities in and from the air, to influence the behaviour of actors and the course of events."
  • Air Capability: Anything that travels through the air, including aircraft, platforms, and weapons (such as air-to-air missiles).
  • Course of Events: The outcome of friendly or enemy operations or activity in the operational theatre.

2. Levels of Warfare & National Security Strategy

ConceptDoctrinal Definition
Strategic LevelHigh-level national planning set out by the UK Government to achieve a specific overarching aim.
Operational Level (JFAC)How military forces in an operational theatre are employed and coordinated to achieve the strategic goals.
Tactical LevelThe specific individual operations and sorties that make up the events of the conflict.
Protect, Project, PromoteProtect UK people, territory & airspace; Project global influence rapidly & decisively; Promote prosperity & alliances.

3. Principle Strengths & Limitations of Air Power

5 Principle Strengths3 Principle Limitations
• Height: Avoids surface threats, enables 3D manoeuvre, and provides Perspective (wide radar/visual horizon).
• Speed: Rapid response to crisis and element of surprise.
• Reach: Crosses land and sea barriers to reach any point on Earth.
• Agility: Adapts rapidly across strategic, operational, and tactical tasks (dynamic re-tasking).
• Ubiquity: Combination of Height, Speed & Reach—appearing present everywhere at once. 		• Impermanence: Aircraft cannot stay in the air indefinitely; they cannot hold ground and must land to refuel (reduced via Air-to-Air Refuelling).

• Limited Payload: Aircraft carry far less weight/munitions than ships or land vehicles (reduced via precision weapons & rapid re-arming).

• Vulnerability (Fragility): Aircraft are fragile and easily destroyed, especially when parked on the ground (reduced via Passive/Active DCA & RAF Regiment protection).
Commander's Golden Rules (LO3 Doctrine):
1. Aircraft Cannot Occupy Land: Independent air operations never have the same potency as Joint Operations (Air, Land, and Maritime working under a single command structure).
2. Full Spectrum Approach: True crisis resolution requires combining Military, Diplomatic, and Economic levers of power.

Station 2: Control of the Air Cell

Your Role: You are responsible for gaining and maintaining Control of the Air—determining how freely friendly forces can move through operational airspace without prohibitive interference from the enemy.

1. The 3 Degrees of Control of the Air

Degree of ControlDoctrinal MeaningOperational Impact
1. Favourable Air Situation The lowest degree of dominance (localized in time and space) where some enemy interference is still to be expected. Slow transports, tankers, and helicopters are at high risk if unescorted.
2. Air Superiority A degree of dominance of one force over another that permits the conduct of operations by the dominating force at a given time and place without prohibitive interference. Strike and ground-support missions can proceed; localized SAM/fighter threats may still exist.
3. Air Supremacy When the opposing air force is incapable of effective interference anywhere in the theatre. Total freedom of manoeuvre for all air, land, and maritime forces.

2. Counter-Air Operations (OCA vs. DCA)

  • Offensive Counter-Air (OCA): Proactive missions that target and destroy enemy air capabilities (fighters, runways, radar sites, Surface-to-Air Missiles) as close to their source as possible to prevent threats from launching.
  • Defensive Counter-Air (DCA): Protecting friendly forces and bases from enemy air/missile attack using two methods:
    • Active DCA Measures: Detection, Interception, and Engagement using air-to-air fighters (Typhoon Quick Reaction Alert / CAP) and Surface-to-Air Missiles (e.g., Type 45 Destroyer Sea Viper).
    • Passive DCA Measures: Defence and protection on the ground—Hardening (Hardened Aircraft Shelters), Dispersal of aircraft across airfields, Camouflage, Concealment, and Deception (decoys), Early Warning, and Stealth.
  • Anti-Surface Operations for Control of the Air: Using Air Interdiction, Battlefield Air Interdiction (BAI), and Close Air Support to neutralize enemy ground forces (such as mobile SAM resupply convoys) that threaten our aircraft.

3. Key Combat & Air Defence Assets

PlatformRole, Capability & Basing
Typhoon FGR44th-generation multi-role combat aircraft (based at RAF Coningsby & RAF Lossiemouth). Capable of engaging multiple target types: QRA/DCA interception and swing-role air-to-surface attack.
F-35B Lightning5th-generation multi-role combat aircraft (based at RAF Marham & Queen Elizabeth-class Aircraft Carriers). Features Stealth and STOVL to penetrate dense Anti-Access/Area Denial (A2AD) radar defences.
E-3D Sentry / AEW&CAirborne Early Warning and Control System (AWACS). Uses high altitude (Height & Perspective) to look down into mountain valleys and detect low-flying adversary aircraft.

Station 3: Air Attack & Strike Cell

Your Role: You employ kinetic weapons and non-kinetic technologies to shape the battlefield across three core objectives: to Deter adversaries, Disrupt activities, and Destroy enemy resources and capabilities.

1. The 4 Categories of Air Attack

CategorySubtypes & Doctrinal Definitions
1. Strategic Attack Targets the key leadership, command structures, or war-making organisations of an adversary. Directly affects the adversary's ability and will to wage war.
2. Counter-Land Operations
(Force Multipliers)		 • Close Air Support (CAS): Occurs in close proximity to friendly ground forces, complementing ground attack. Requires strict de-confliction (via JTACs) to avoid friendly fire.
• Air Interdiction (AI): Carried out at significant distances behind enemy lines to destroy or delay enemy forces and supplies before they reach the battlefield.
• Strike, Coordination & Reconnaissance (SCAR): A hybrid of AI, CAS, and ISR where combat aircraft detect, coordinate reconnaissance, and attack targets.
3. Counter-Sea Operations • Anti-Submarine Warfare (ASW): Using airborne acoustic sensors (sonobuoys), radar, and torpedoes to locate, track, and attack enemy submarines.
• Anti-Surface Warfare (ASuW): Supporting maritime forces to detect and engage enemy surface warships.
4. Information Activities
(Non-Kinetic Attack)		 • Electronic Warfare (EW): Denies the enemy freedom of access across the electromagnetic spectrum (jamming radars/comms) while protecting friendly access.
• Psychological Operations (PsyOps): Influences adversary behaviour without kinetic destruction—using continuous RPAS presence overhead, high-speed fast-jet "Shows of Force", dropping informational leaflets (as in Op Shader), and radio broadcasts.

2. Three Factors Determining Air Attack Success

  1. Penetration: The ability of aircraft (via Stealth/EW) or stand-off weapons (e.g., Storm Shadow) to penetrate enemy air defences.
  2. Payload: The number of weapons an aircraft can carry (overcoming Limited Payload using precision-guided munitions).
  3. Weather: Poor weather (cloud/sandstorms) can blind optical/laser guidance, requiring all-weather radar/GPS weapons (e.g., Brimstone).

3. Maritime Patrol & Strike Fleet

  • P-8A Poseidon MRA1 (RAF Lossiemouth): Multi-role maritime patrol aircraft equipped with high-resolution mapping radar, acoustic sensor systems (sonobuoys), and torpedoes for Counter-Sea (ASW/ASuW).
  • Royal Navy Wildcat: Fleet Air Arm maritime attack helicopter used for Anti-Surface and Anti-Submarine warfare alongside Royal Navy destroyers and frigates.

Station 4: ISR & Cyber Cell (No 1 ISR Wing)

Your Role: You lead Intelligence, Surveillance, and Reconnaissance (ISR), manage Network Enabled Capability (NEC), and execute Air-Cyber Integration.

1. Core ISR & Network Definitions

  • Intelligence: Timely, accurate information processed and disseminated to support decision-making and build situational awareness.
  • Surveillance: Where a wide area of interest is continually and systematically observed by visual, aural, electronic, or photographic means.
  • Reconnaissance: Where a specific area of interest is observed to gain specific information about specific activities.
  • Network Enabled Capability (NEC): Joining sensors, decision-makers, effectors, and support capabilities in a network (e.g., Link-16) to get the right information to the right place at the right time.

2. The 7 Methods of Intelligence Gathering

MethodDoctrinal Meaning
SIGINTSignals Intelligence: Intercepting adversary communications and electronic signals.
MASINTMeasurement & Signature Intelligence: Gathered from nuclear, radar, geophysical, materials, and radiofrequency signatures.
GEOINTGeospatial Intelligence: Terrain, mapping, and overhead reconnaissance imagery data.
OSINTOpen-Source Intelligence: Publicly available information from the internet, social media, scientific sources, and interviews.
TECHINTTechnical Intelligence: Information gained from analysing captured enemy weapons, hardware, and equipment.
HUMINTHuman Intelligence: Information gathered directly from people, informants, and interrogations.
FININTFinancial Intelligence: Analysis of monetary transactions and illicit banking flows.

3. The 9-Step ISR Process & Fleet Assets

The 9-Step ISR Cycle: 1. Request for Information (Aim to gain specific info) → 2. Plan → 3. Task/Re-task → 4. Collect → 5. Analyse → 6. Disseminate (Pass to those who need to know) → 7. Evaluate → 8. Apply → 9. Requirement Satisfied.
4 Factors for ISR Success: Obtaining timely accurate info; processing/analysing it; distributing results to decision-makers; and making the decision.
  • RC-135W Rivet Joint (RAF Waddington): Dedicated electronic surveillance aircraft surveying the electromagnetic spectrum for SIGINT and MASINT.
  • Reaper MQ-9A / Protector RPAS (RAF Waddington): Remotely piloted Medium-Altitude Long-Endurance (MALE) aircraft providing persistent surveillance sensors and precision attack.
  • Shadow R1 (RAF Waddington): Electro-optical and electronic intelligence gathering for ground commanders.
  • Zephyr HAPS: Solar-powered stratospheric platform exploiting Height and Perspective for weeks of persistent surveillance.
  • Air-Cyber Integration: Defensive Air-Cyber protects friendly networks; Offensive Air-Cyber disables enemy air defence radar networks (as in Operation Orchard, 2007) so vulnerable aircraft can penetrate hostile airspace safely.

Station 5: Air Mobility & Logistics Cell

Your Role: You manage the transportation of personnel, equipment, and fuel between and within theatres to project and sustain Air Power.

1. The 6 Divisions of Air Mobility

DivisionKey Terminology & Doctrinal Definitions
1. Hub & Spoke Operations Creates an "air bridge" linking main bases in the UK (Hubs, e.g., RAF Brize Norton) to forward bases closer to theatre (Spokes, e.g., RAF Akrotiri).
• Inter-Theatre: Movement between different theatres (Strategic transports: C-17, Voyager, A400M).
• Intra-Theatre: Movement within a theatre (Tactical transports/rotary: Chinook, Puma, Hercules).
2. Airborne Operations • Air-Drop: Requires a parachute to deliver troops or loads whilst the aircraft is in flight.
• Air-Land: The aircraft lands on a runway to deliver its load.
3. Air-to-Air Refuelling (AAR) Refuelling aircraft in flight (via Voyager) to overcome Air Power's limitation of Impermanence—extending Reach, Endurance, and Flexibility (famously proven by Victor K2 tankers in Operation Black Buck, 1982).
4. Aeromedical Evacuation • Forward Medevac: Moving injured personnel from the battlefield to initial medical treatment facilities (e.g., Chinook/Puma MERT).
• Tactical Medevac: Moving patients between medical facilities in or close to theatre.
• Strategic Medevac: Moving seriously ill or injured personnel from the theatre medical facility back to the UK (e.g., C-17).
5. Special Forces Air Operations Using specialist aircraft and crews to enable covert insertion, sustainment, and extraction of UK Special Forces (e.g., Operation Mikado).
6. Personnel Recovery • Peacetime Recovery: Rescue operations where there is no threat posed by hostile interference.
• Combat Recovery: Time-sensitive operations to rescue isolated/captured personnel where hostile interference is expected (practised in Exercise Volfa).

2. Air Mobility & Rotary Fleet

  • C-17 Globemaster (RAF Brize Norton | Range: 4,500nm): Long-range heavy-lift strategic transport and Strategic Medevac.
  • A400M Atlas C.1 (RAF Brize Norton | Range: 4,100nm): Carries 116 troops, Chinooks, vehicles, or palleted loads for Air-Land and Air-Drop.
  • Voyager (RAF Brize Norton | Range: 8,000nm): Air-to-Air Refuelling (AAR) tanker and strategic passenger transport.
  • Chinook & Puma HC2 (Joint Helicopter Command): Highly versatile support helicopters for Intra-Theatre troop transport, underslung artillery loads, Combat Recovery, and Forward CASEVAC/MERT (Puma fits up to 6 stretchers).

Station 6: Joint Action & Multi-Domain Cell

Your Role: You coordinate the 4 Enablers of Air Power, Cross-Domain Integration (Land, Maritime, Space), Types of Operations, and the Full Spectrum Approach.

1. The 4 Factors Enabling Air Power

EnablerDoctrinal Meaning & Real-World Example
1. BaseWell-positioned bases (e.g., RAF Akrotiri or Aircraft Carriers) providing maximum operational agility.
2. SustainAir Logistics maintaining supply chains so forces remain operational and on task for as long as possible.
3. ConnectSecure communications (e.g., RAF Signals units) enabling information transfer and interoperability.
4. ProtectProtection of bases, assets, information, and personnel (e.g., RAF Regiment providing airfield Force Protection).

2. Joint Action & Cross-Domain Integration

  • Joint Operation: Two or more services (RAF, Royal Navy, British Army) operating as a single military force with a single command structure. Independent air operations lack the potency of joint operations because aircraft cannot occupy land.
  • Full Spectrum Approach: Multi-agency crisis management combining Military, Diplomatic, and Economic approaches.
  • Air-Land Integration (ALI): Processes that plan, coordinate, control, and de-conflict air firepower and land forces in a shared battlespace.
  • Air-Maritime Integration (AMI): Coordinating ISR (Poseidon) and combat air with naval warships (Type 45 Destroyers / Aircraft Carriers), especially vital against Anti-Access/Area Denial (A2AD) threats.
  • Air-Space Integration: Space power is vital for enabling Positioning, Navigation, and Timing (PNT / GPS) for aircraft and precision weapons, as well as beyond-line-of-sight voice and data communications.

3. Key Types of Military Operations (Task 9)

  • Conflict Prevention & Defence Diplomacy: Using foreign policy and defence resources/capabilities to establish peace.
  • Preventative Diplomacy: Preventing disputes from escalating into conflict or limiting the spread of an occurring conflict.
  • Deterrence vs. Coercion: Deterrence provides a threat to prevent an adversary from attacking; Coercion uses a threat or force to compel an opponent to change their behaviour.
  • Destruction, Denial & Disruption: Destruction destroys specific assets; Denial makes it difficult for an enemy to achieve an objective; Disruption delays or prevents planned enemy operations.
  • Peacekeeping vs. Peace Enforcement: Peacekeeping prevents fighting from resuming after a conflict; Peace Enforcement uses military force to compel peace during a conflict without consent from the participants.
  • Post-Conflict Peace Building & Humanitarian Ops: Laying foundations for sustainable peace; and providing short-term material and logistical aid to people in need after natural or man-made disasters.
